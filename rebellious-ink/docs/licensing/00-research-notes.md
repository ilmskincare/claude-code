# Research notes: Birmingham skin piercing and tattoo registration

Prepared for Rebellious Ink. All sources were checked on 4 November 2026. These notes are working evidence for the pack. They are not legal advice. Where something could not be confirmed this is said plainly, and the pack carries a bracketed placeholder instead of a guess.

England is the jurisdiction throughout. Where Wales or Scotland differ, the England position applies to the studio.

## 1. What I confirmed about Birmingham

**Source:** Birmingham City Council, "Register to provide acupuncture, tattooing, piercing or electrolysis treatments". URL: https://www.birmingham.gov.uk/info/20083/general_licensing_applications/292/register_to_provide_skin-piercing_treatments (page shows "Page last updated: 02 August 2024"; checked 4 November 2026).

- To offer skin-piercing treatments as a business, both the premises and the person performing the treatment must be registered with the council, under the Local Government (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1982. The treatments listed are acupuncture, cosmetic piercing (ear and body), electrolysis, semi-permanent skin colouring and tattooing.
- The council states it has no powers to refuse registration, but practice is controlled through compliance with byelaws.
- The certificate of registration and a copy of the byelaws must be displayed at the premises.
- Registration stays in force until the business ceases to exist or the registration is cancelled. A practitioner must submit a new registration if they move to different premises.
- The council aims to process applications within 20 working days, and registration documents can take up to 10 more working days to be issued.
- Application is online through the council licensing portal, using a BRUM account (or by creating one). The portal link on the page is labelled "Register online to provide skin-piercing treatment". The council also publishes a portal user guide.
- The online form can only be used for a single applicant (practitioner) and a single treatment type. A form with multiple applicants or multiple treatment types is rejected. A separate application and fee is needed for each practitioner and each treatment type.
- Enquiries: licensing@birmingham.gov.uk (General licensing enquiry).

**Source:** Birmingham City Council, Licensing Schedule of Fees and Charges, "Other licences". URL: https://www.birmingham.gov.uk/info/20083/general_licensing_applications/210/licensing_schedule_of_fees_and_charges/5 (checked 4 November 2026).

- Under "Skin piercers": Skin piercing treatment, **£80**. Copy licence, **£30**. The schedule notes that skin piercing covers acupuncture, cosmetic piercing, electrolysis, semi-permanent skin colouring and tattooing.
- The schedule does not state a financial year on the extract I read.

**Could not confirm for Birmingham:**

- Whether a separate premises fee applies in addition to the £80 per practitioner per treatment type. The fee schedule lists one line only. Ask the licensing team.
- A form reference number or code. The online portal form is the only route I found, and I did not find a named paper form.
- The text of Birmingham's adopted byelaws. I could not find a Birmingham byelaw document online. The council page says a copy of the byelaws must be displayed, so the council will supply it. The pack uses the Department of Health model byelaws (below) as the working standard and asks the studio to replace that with the Birmingham text once received.
- Whether apprentices who perform part of a procedure under supervision must each hold their own registration. The council page says the person performing the treatment must be registered. The pack treats apprentices as needing registration before they perform any part of a procedure, and asks the studio to confirm with the council.
- What the inspection visit involves. I found no Birmingham-specific inspection guidance. Section 17 of the 1982 Act and the CIEH toolkit audit tool are the basis for the "what the officer will want to see" list in document 01, and that list is stated as expectation, not as Birmingham policy.

## 2. The statute

**Local Government (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1982, Part VIII, sections 15 and 16.**
URLs: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1982/30/section/15 and https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1982/30/section/16 (checked 4 November 2026).

- Section 15 covers tattooing, semi-permanent skin-colouring, cosmetic piercing and electrolysis. A person may not carry on these businesses unless registered, and may not do so except from premises registered for the purpose. The authority may charge reasonable fees. The authority cannot require information about individual clients on the application. The section does not apply to work by, or under the supervision of, a registered medical practitioner.
- Section 15(7) is the byelaw power: cleanliness of premises and fittings, cleanliness of registered persons and their assistants, and cleansing and, so far as appropriate, sterilisation of instruments, materials and equipment.
- Section 16: breach of section 15(1) or (2), or of a byelaw, is a summary offence, maximum level 3 on the standard scale. Failing to display the certificate and byelaws is a summary offence, maximum level 2. A court that convicts for a byelaw breach may suspend or cancel the registration, and may extend that to the premises registration if the offender occupies the premises. There is a defence of all reasonable precautions and all due diligence.
- Section 17 (powers of entry) was named in search results but I did not fetch it, so the pack does not quote it.

## 3. The byelaws and the CIEH toolkit

**Model byelaws.** URL: https://www.cieh.org/media/1958/tattoo-toolkit_part-b_appendix-01.pdf (read in full, 4 November 2026). These are the model byelaws for acupuncture, tattooing, semi-permanent skin-colouring, cosmetic piercing and electrolysis. They deal with three things, which match the three headings in the brief:

1. Cleanliness of premises and fittings (byelaw 2): walls, floors, ceilings kept clean and in repair; waste handled lawfully; needles single-use where practicable; surfaces that may be contaminated smooth, impervious and disinfected after each client and at the end of each working day; couch covered with a disposable paper sheet changed for each client; no eating, drinking or smoking in the treatment area with notices displayed; a treatment area used solely for treatment; a smooth impervious floor in the treatment area.
2. Cleansing and sterilisation of instruments, materials and equipment (byelaw 3): clothing and coverings clean and not reused between clients unless adequately cleansed; needles and instruments sterile; dye sterile and inert; dye containers disposed of after each treatment or cleaned and sterilised; adequate cleansing and sterilisation facilities (unless only pre-sterilised items are used); gas and electrical outlets; constant hot and cold water; clean storage.
3. Cleanliness of persons (byelaw 4): clean hands and short nails; open lesions covered with impermeable dressing; new disposable gloves for each client; clean washable or disposable protective clothing; no smoking, eating or drinking in the treatment area; hand-washing facilities for the sole use of operators with hot and cold water and soap; sanitary accommodation for operators.

Byelaw 5 says registered persons who visit clients at their request must observe the operator requirements.

**Tattooing and body piercing guidance toolkit.** URL: https://www.cieh.org/media/2004/tattooing-and-body-piercing-guidance-toolkit-july-2013.pdf (read via text extraction, 4 November 2026). ISBN 978-1-906989-72-9, dated July 2013, produced with Public Health England (the predecessor of UKHSA), endorsed by the CIEH. Part A covers legislation, standard infection control principles, hand hygiene, PPE, sharps and blood exposure, waste, environmental cleaning, record keeping, consent and aftercare. Part B has appendices including the model byelaws, a consent form template, aftercare follow-up record sheet, equipment decontamination requirements, a sterilisation self-assessment tool and autoclave daily record sheet. It also covers hepatitis B immunisation, and says antibody levels should be checked one to four months after the primary course and a single booster offered about five years later to those at continued risk.

- **Currency caveat.** The toolkit is from 2013. I did not find a newer UKHSA edition. I treat it as the main reference that councils still point applicants to, and I did not confirm that it has not been replaced. Check the CIEH and GOV.UK sites for a newer edition before submitting.
- It says the Tattooing of Minors Act 1969 sets a statutory minimum age of 18 for tattooing, that the practitioner has a defence of good reason to believe the client was 18 or over, and that the consent of an under-18 is not a defence.
- It says (2013) there is no statutory minimum age for other skin piercing in England outside local rules, and that ear and nose piercing of a minor is generally acceptable with a parent or guardian giving consent and being present.
- It recommends client records be kept on the registered premises for no less than 3 years.
- It says employers must pay for protective measures such as immunisation under the Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974, and that practitioners who refuse hepatitis B vaccination should be asked to sign a disclaimer.

## 4. Age and consent law

- **Tattooing of Minors Act 1969, section 1.** URL: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1969/24/section/1 (checked 4 November 2026). "It shall be an offence to tattoo a person under the age of eighteen." Exception: medical reasons by a duly qualified medical practitioner or under their direction. Defence: reasonable cause to believe the person was 18 or over, and actually believing it. The penalty did not appear on the page I read. The CIEH toolkit states up to level 3, which I could not check against the Act directly. Extent: England, Wales and Scotland.
- **Wales intimate piercing.** Public Health (Wales) Act 2017, sections 95 and 96, Part 5. URL: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/anaw/2017/2/part/5/crossheading/offences-related-to-intimate-piercing (checked 4 November 2026). It is an offence for a person in Wales to perform an intimate piercing on someone under 18, or to make arrangements in Wales to do so. Intimate body parts listed: anus, breast (including nipple and areola), buttock, natal cleft, penis (including foreskin), perineum, pubic mound, scrotum, tongue, vulva. Defences include reasonable steps to establish age, such as asking for evidence of age a reasonable person would accept. In force 1 February 2018. This confirms the brief: the statutory offence is Welsh only.
- **England.** I found no English statute creating an equivalent offence and no statutory minimum age for body piercing in England (the 2013 toolkit says the same; a secondary search summary agreed). Absence of a rule in a search is not proof, so treat this as "none found". The studio policy of 18+ for intimate piercings is therefore a studio rule, not an English legal requirement, and the pack says so. The pack borrows the Welsh list of intimate body parts as the studio's definition because it is the only statutory list I could find.
- Consent for under-18s: the toolkit says courts have held that under-18s may consent to cosmetic body piercing if sufficiently mature, and that this is a judgment for the operator. The studio policy does not rely on that: ear and nose only for 16 and 17 year olds, with a parent or guardian present in person.

## 5. Tattoo ink and the REACH restriction

- **EU.** Entry 75 of Annex XVII to REACH, added by Commission Regulation (EU) 2020/2081, has applied since 4 January 2022. I took the 2022 start date from the HSE consultation text and a secondary summary; I did not fetch EUR-Lex.
- **Great Britain.** The Defra restriction decision dated 30 December 2025, published on GOV.UK, states that there is no legislation in GB regulating which substances may be present in tattoo ink and permanent make-up, and that the Secretary of State is proposing a draft amendment to Annex 17 of UK REACH. URL: https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6965116396e60a090ce1ff7f/UK-REACH-restriction-report-substances-in-tattoo-inks-permanent_make-up.pdf (read in full, 4 November 2026). It follows the HSE preferred option with stricter concentration limits, removes derogations for 19 pigments (including Pigment Blue 15:3 and Pigment Green 7), and adds labelling requirements including allergen warnings for nickel and chromium (VI). The agency option proposed a two-year transition for formulators and suppliers and one further year for artists to use up non-compliant ink. A draft amendment is published at https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/69651187e8b93f59c3aeccf5/UK-REACH-draft-amendment-substances-in-tattoo-inks-permanent_make-up.pdf, which I did not read in full.
- **Not confirmed:** whether the GB amendment has been made, laid or brought into force by 4 November 2026, and the final transition dates. Secondary sources disagree on the transition periods. The studio policy therefore says: buy only inks whose supplier confirms in writing compliance with the EU Entry 75 limits, which is the stricter practical standard, and review the policy when the GB regulations are confirmed.

## 6. Waste

- Duty of care for waste is section 34 of the Environmental Protection Act 1990, which applies to anyone who produces, holds, carries or disposes of controlled waste in Great Britain (stated in several secondary sources; I did not fetch the Act).
- Clinical waste that is hazardous is carried under a hazardous waste consignment note, not a simple transfer note. England and Wales: Hazardous Waste (England and Wales) Regulations 2005. GOV.UK guidance: https://www.gov.uk/hazardous-waste-consignment-note-supplementary-guidance and the GOV.UK page "Hazardous waste" (only snippets reached me). Secondary sources give three years as the retention period for consignment notes and two years for transfer notes. I could not confirm these from GOV.UK.
- **Not confirmed:** whether premises that produce hazardous waste must register with the Environment Agency (secondary sources are inconsistent), and which regulations now govern transfer notes (two secondary sources disagreed about the 1991 Duty of Care Regulations versus the Waste (England and Wales) Regulations 2011). The contractor must confirm both. The pack's policy sets a seven year retention for waste records, which exceeds every figure I found and is simple to apply.
- NHS "Safe management of healthcare waste" (2012) is the usual reference for colour coding. I only saw a fragment, so it is cited as a pointer, not relied on.

## 7. Health and safety, COSHH, RIDDOR

- **COSHH.** HSE pages, e.g. https://www.hse.gov.uk/coshh/basics/assessment.htm and https://www.hse.gov.uk/coshh/basics/whatdo.htm (via search extract, 4 November 2026): employers must assess risks from hazardous substances, prevent or adequately control exposure, and give information, instruction and training. Employers with five or more employees must record significant findings. A safety data sheet is not a substitute for an assessment.
- **RIDDOR.** HSE pages, e.g. https://www.hse.gov.uk/riddor/types-of-reportable-incidents.htm and https://www.hse.gov.uk/biosafety/blood-borne-viruses/incident-reporting.htm (fetched 4 November 2026). Over-seven-day injuries to workers must be reported within 15 days of the accident. For blood-borne viruses: regulation 7 (dangerous occurrence, which includes a sharps injury involving known infected blood or body fluid), regulation 4(2) (over-seven-day absence) and regulation 9(b) (disease from occupational exposure). Absence of more than three consecutive days must be recorded but not reported. The responsible person is the employer or person in control of premises.
- **Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974.** Cited via the toolkit (section on general duties). I did not fetch the Act. Whether each artist is an employee or self-employed changes who holds which duty, so document 09 carries a placeholder for employment status.

## 8. UK GDPR, client records and retention

- ICO, "Storage limitation". URL: https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/data-protection-principles/a-guide-to-the-data-protection-principles/storage-limitation/ (search extract, 4 November 2026). The UK GDPR sets no fixed retention periods. The controller decides and must be able to justify the period, document it in a retention schedule, and delete or anonymise data it no longer needs. Data can be kept while a legal claim could still arise.
- Limitation Act 1980, section 2: six years for actions founded on tort. URL: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1980/58/section/2 (fetched 4 November 2026). This is the reasoning behind the recommended **seven years** (six years plus a year of margin). It is a studio recommendation, not a statutory duty. The CIEH toolkit minimum is three years. The studio should confirm the period with its insurer.
- Health information in consent and screening forms is special category data under UK GDPR. I did not fetch an ICO page for this, so the point is flagged for the studio to confirm. Whether the studio must pay the ICO data protection fee is also unconfirmed here.
- Section 15(5) of the 1982 Act prevents the council from asking for information about individual clients on the application. Client records are inspected on site, they are not sent to the council.

## 9. Deposits and consumer protection

- CMA unfair contract terms guidance (CMA37): https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6a609329b00f3323bf1a23f3/unfair_contract_terms_guidance.pdf (search extract, 4 November 2026). A deposit can be kept in full only where it is a small percentage of the price and it is clear when it becomes non-refundable. Terms making prepayments non-refundable regardless of why the customer cancels are unlikely to be fair. Cancellation charges should reflect a genuine estimate of loss. Unfair terms do not bind the consumer.
- The 14-day cancellation right in the Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013 for distance contracts was not confirmed from a primary source in this research. The pack asks the studio to display deposit and cancellation terms at booking and to check the position with Trading Standards or Citizens Advice for online bookings.
