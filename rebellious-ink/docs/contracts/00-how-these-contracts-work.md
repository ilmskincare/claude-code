## 1. The risk, honestly

You want artists to be self-employed. You also want them to answer enquiries quickly, turn up, pay daily, get posts approved and bring in business, or the arrangement may end. Each is reasonable. Together they can lead a court or HMRC to say the artist is really a **worker** or an **employee**, whatever the contract says.

UK law has employees, workers (some rights, such as minimum wage and paid holiday) and the genuinely self-employed. A label does not settle it: courts look at how the arrangement works in practice (*Autoclenz v Belcher*, 2011). The main tests:

- **Personal service:** must the person do the work themselves, or can they send someone else?
- **Control:** how far does the studio decide what is done, when and how?
- **Mutuality of obligation:** is there an expectation that work is offered and must be done?

These come from *Ready Mixed Concrete v Minister of Pensions* (1968). Two Supreme Court cases show how they bite today. In *Pimlico Plumbers v Smith* (2018) a plumber labelled self-employed was a worker: his right to send a substitute was too narrow, he wore the company's uniform and drove its van, and he was tied closely to the business. In *Uber v Aslam* (2021) drivers were workers because the company controlled the price, the allocation of jobs and the terms, and the written terms did not decide it.

For you, the tension is plain. **Approving posts** is control over presentation. **Requiring reliability** looks like an obligation to work. **Ending for about eight weeks of low activity** looks like a minimum-output rule. **Daily accounting** looks like close supervision. If an arrangement is found to be employment or worker status, the studio could owe tax and National Insurance, holiday pay, minimum wage top-ups and pension contributions, going back years. HMRC's **Check Employment Status for Tax (CEST)** tool is the standard first test. It covers tax and National Insurance, not every employment right, so it is a start and not the end.

## 2. How the drafts keep the self-employed character

Each artist contract (01 to 04) is a **licence to use the studio's premises, booking system and brand**. It is not an instruction on how to do the work. The studio's expectations are conditions of that licence.

- **The artist runs their own business.** They have their own tax, National Insurance, public liability insurance (at least [£5 million]), council registration certificate and kit.
- **They set their own prices** within their published rate card, **choose which enquiries to accept**, and can decline any with no penalty.
- **No obligation to offer or accept work.** The studio promises no bookings. The artist promises no attendance. Agreed days are days the chair is held, not days they must work.
- **They can send a substitute** who is suitably qualified, registered and insured, with the studio's consent. Consent can be refused only on objective grounds (registration, insurance, hygiene, safety).
- **Reliability** is written as **brand standards**: reply within [24] hours (accept, decline or hand back), and turn up to accepted bookings or give [48] hours' notice.
- **Post approval** covers only content using the studio's name, logo, premises or hashtag. The artist's own brand is theirs, and the manager cannot refuse on taste.
- **The eight-week rule** is a **review of the licence**, not a quota. Either side can end it on [7] days' notice after a meeting. No fee, penalty or discipline follows from low activity.
- **Daily settlement** is a payment term for money owed, in both directions. Late payment attracts interest and, after [7] days, suspension of the licence.
- **The apprentice (05) is different.** An apprentice is usually not self-employed in substance. The draft makes the status choice explicit and flags minimum wage for decision.

**The drafting only helps if the studio behaves accordingly.** Do not set prices, rotas or targets. Do not penalise declined enquiries or run appraisals. Let people use substitutes and take time off. If practice drifts from the paper, the paper will not protect you.

## 4. Before anyone signs

1. **Run each arrangement through HMRC CEST** (GOV.UK, "Check employment status for tax"). Answer for how the person will really work, not for what you hope. **Save the result and the answers with the date.** Repeat it if the arrangement changes.
2. **Ask your accountant** to confirm tax, National Insurance and VAT treatment, and the commission base. The studio's working assumptions are in `docs/01-blocking-questions.md`.
3. **Have a solicitor review** all of it, especially the status clauses, the indemnity clauses marked optional, the interest wording and the slot-deposit forfeiture.
4. **For apprentices, decide status and pay first** (agreement 05), with advice.
5. Check what is in force on the signing date. Employment law is changing (including under the Employment Rights Act 2025).
